Field Note 08 · Art · Exarchia, Athens · By Maor Harel

PAME GALLERY

A gallery programme is not a list of artists. It is a sequence of decisions about who belongs in the room, how the work is installed, who comes through the door and what happens after opening night.

The setting

Pame Gallery operated in Exarchia, a neighbourhood inseparable from Athens street-art culture. That context mattered. A gallery working with contemporary and street artists cannot treat the neighbourhood as a decorative address. The programme has to understand the scene it is entering.

The work included exhibition programming, artist relations, production and installation, openings and sales. One of the projects was a pop-up with The Krah, an Athens writer whose work developed from the city’s graffiti culture and extended internationally.

The published Dubel Team record describes Pame Gallery as contemporary and street-art programming in Exarchia, covering artist relations, production, install, openings and sales.

What we actually owned

Gallery work looks quiet from outside because much of the operating friction is hidden. Artists need clear communication. Works have physical constraints. Install has deadlines. Opening night changes the room entirely. Sales conversations require a different tone from event promotion.

The programme therefore had to hold several relationships at once: with the artist, the work, the audience, the neighbourhood and the commercial reality of the gallery. None of those can be reduced to social content without losing credibility.

What made it hard

Street art becomes easy to flatten when it enters a gallery. The challenge is giving work a serious presentation without removing the context that made it meaningful. That requires restraint in curation and production. The room should help people see the work, not perform importance around it.

What stayed with us

Pame reinforced the importance of context as an operating asset. The same object, event or message can feel completely different depending on where and how it is presented. That lesson later carried into museum work, hospitality programming and product design: environment changes interpretation.
