Breath as a Weapon

4 min read

January 2021 · Jaffa

The British variant arrived like a sequel no one had bought a ticket for. Suddenly all the familiar scenes were back, only in a faster, more contagious version. Alex, still unvaccinated, found himself positive after yet another underground party in the middle of the third lockdown – a street party where the music turned up his heart but wasn't enough to protect his body.

His small flat in Jaffa, with its grey walls, bare furniture and cold floor tiles, became, overnight, a fortress he was forbidden by law to leave. A modern prison. He stood by the window and watched the raindrops beat against the glass, as if each of them carried a message written in advance: this is where your freedom ends.

Minute after minute, hour after hour, ten days of isolation piled up like stones in a tower he wasn't sure he could bear. The food deliveries left outside his door – paper bags of vegetables and pasta, cartons of ready-made salads – became social events. Every knock on the door was a moment of human contact: short, snatched, essential.

"Look," he whispered to himself in the mirror, his gaze fixed on his own grey-green eyes. "Mild symptoms. Strong immune system. Not luck, not chance. That's me."

The words sounded to him like an inner password, a seal on his strength. Proof that he could survive, overcome, keep moving.

But on the third night his chest tightened, and the air wouldn't go all the way in. He lay on his back and counted his breaths, the way they had taught him on the range in Golani: four seconds in, four seconds out, hold for a moment before the squeeze of the trigger. Breath as a weapon. That was how, in the end, he fell asleep.

The family video call didn't change the feeling. Irina and Vitaly appeared on the screen, their eyes full of worry, trying to break through the digital barrier that wouldn't let them really be there. Behind them, in the corner of the frame, Natalie sat with her phone in her hand, pretending not to listen. Their voices came through small speakers, slightly thick, and hung in the air like a vapour of helplessness.

"I'm leaving," Alex announced, and his voice was strong and piercing, despite his shortness of breath. "When Greece opens to tourists, it won't ask for a vaccination certificate. A negative test will do. I can really live there."

In the corner of the screen, Natalie looked up from her phone.

Irina shuddered in front of the camera. "Alex… you don't understand," she said quietly, almost in a whisper. "It's not just the virus. All the apps, all the tracking, all the rules – they're building a system of control there's no escaping. There too."

Alex gave a bitter half-smile, and his eyes wandered to the small painting on the wall, which reminded him of the days before lockdown. "It's a different kind of control," he said. "I'm swapping one kind of surveillance for another. But at least I get to choose where I am."

The decision had been ripening in him for months: watching the news, reading reports, a personal experiment on the thin line between freedom and escape. And now, with the Green Pass already at the door and a code on his phone about to decide where he was allowed to go, he understood that for him freedom was not a physical measure. It was an inner feeling, complex and delicate.

"I won't accept rules I had no part in writing," he thought. "I won't live a life someone else wrote for me."

At night, when the city was almost silent – only distant cars and the tap of a closed umbrella on the pavement – he sat by the window and looked down at the empty street. Sometimes a young couple passed, masks on their faces, the two of them watching each other with a gentle mistrust.

"That's us. That's the world now," he whispered. "People living between screens, between rules, between fear and freedom."

But inside all those grey thoughts there was a small light. He saw himself in Greece – not just another country, but a springboard. He pictured the narrow streets of Athens, Psiri, the Aegean, the small flat near the Ancient Agora.

"There," he whispered, "I can breathe."

His phone screen went dark on the windowsill. The small room stayed quiet. Alex took a deep breath, hugged himself, and understood: there was no absolute escape. But there was choice. There was a life lying in his own hands, even inside a reality that kept trying to force its rules of the game on him.

End of chapter
