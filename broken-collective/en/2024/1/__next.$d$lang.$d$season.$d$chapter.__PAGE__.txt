12:T3032,<h2 class="scene scene-first" data-i="0">January 2024 · The Gaza border</h2><p data-i="1" class="opener">The skies over Israel had known no rest since that day in October.</p><p data-i="2">Nuni was already in the ground. So was Omer, but in a temporary grave, far from home: Kfar Aza was still too dangerous, and there was no permission to bury anyone there. There was life before the seventh of October, and life after it. In between, there was nothing.</p><p data-i="3">Alex had got onto one of the first flights out of Athens, the day after the massacre, with hundreds of other Israelis who had dropped everything in the middle of their lives to go and report for duty. He was no longer a soldier, but the Golani reserves didn't ask. At the end of October his company went into the Strip, and he hadn't come out of it, not really, for three months.</p><p data-i="4">Now, as January opened its cold gates, he stood in the base showers, the scalding water running over his aching body and failing to wash away what he saw behind his eyelids. Another night without sleep. Another night in which the scars from his regular service mixed with the new things. His hands shook on the soap. Every slammed door, every shout, took him back to the darkest place there is.</p><p data-i="5">But he couldn't fall apart. Natalie needed him. Natalie, who had survived what can't be survived.</p><p data-i="6">Outside, the whole country had mobilised. Mountains of donations arrived at the bases: socks, cigarettes, quiches in foil trays with notes from children. Evacuees from the north and from the Gaza border slept in hotels at the Dead Sea and in Eilat, and Tel Aviv opened its homes to them. World leaders, shocked by the massacre, still let Israel strike with all its force. But the numbers from Gaza rose every day, and every day more voices around the world asked: how long?</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="7">January 2024 · Tel Aviv</h2><p data-i="8">Natalie woke in a cold sweat. The same dream again. The smoke again. Roni's voice again, still reporting into the microphone.</p><p data-i="9">She swung her legs off the bed, and the cold parquet touched the soles of her feet. The room spun for a moment, a side effect of the new pills. She went to the window and looked at Tel Aviv waking up. People going to work, buying coffee, hurrying for the bus. As if it were allowed.</p><p data-i="10">How was she supposed to be normal after what had happened? How was she supposed to see an Arab in the street and not tremble? How was she supposed not to hate?</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="11">January 2024 · Gaza City</h2><p data-i="12">Mehran woke to the sound of the bombing. Again. Always.</p><p data-i="13">The south wall of the house had cracked yesterday, and now every plane that passed overhead threatened to bring the roof down on the small children sleeping on the floor. Three families in one house, nine people in three rooms. Most of the neighbourhood had gone south back in October, when Israel ordered the residents of the northern Strip to evacuate. Ahmed refused. &quot;You don't leave twice,&quot; he said, and he meant his grandfather, who left in 1948 and never came back.</p><p data-i="14">Fatma was still working at al-Shifa, in corridors that had become a refugee camp. She came home at night too quiet, her hands smelling of disinfectant and smoke.</p><p data-i="15">Hunger gnawed at his stomach, and still Mehran couldn't eat. Jana and Reem split one pitta a day between them and laughed so that he wouldn't worry. And he thought about the hostages, somewhere here, under the same ground, hungry too. How do you eat when death hovers over everyone's head?</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="16">February 2024 · Inside the Strip</h2><p data-i="17">The fighting went on for months, and no end was in sight. Round after round of negotiations burned out, like hope. More than a hundred hostages were still in Gaza.</p><p data-i="18">Alex went out on night missions with his heart pounding so hard he was sure everyone could hear it. Every time he passed a ruined building he thought of Mehran, and of everyone hiding in those ruins with their pain. In every scream he heard Natalie. He was torn between duty and humanity, between justice and mercy, between the urge for revenge and compassion.</p><p data-i="19">And one night, without thinking too much, he sprayed the Hapoel ultras' emblem on a burnt wall. Red on grey.</p><p data-i="20">Only when he walked away did he understand what he'd done. He, who had always said that Hapoel was the left, was brotherhood, was everything that stood against wars like this one – he had marked territory in Gaza. But his little sister had been saved by a miracle, and good friends had not come back from that day. The pain had beaten the belief. And maybe the belief itself had already broken: the belief that Arabs and Jews could live side by side, in two states, as neighbours. It had slipped away from him, and in its place was a crisis of identity that kept hardening.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="21">March 2024 · Budapest, on the phone</h2><p data-i="22">When the company came out to rest on the Israeli side, he called Emily straight away.</p><p data-i="23">She had gone back to Budapest in November, to her mother, because alone in Athens she couldn't breathe. The first minutes of the call were full of feeling. She cried, he laughed a tired laugh, and there was a moment when it seemed nothing had changed.</p><p data-i="24">But behind her, Hungarian television was talking. On the screen, a city of ash. Buildings turned into layers of concrete. Children covered in dust, with eyes looking for someone. And the numbers: more than thirty thousand dead, according to the Hamas-run health ministry in Gaza, which doesn't distinguish between fighters and civilians. Israel said thousands of them were militants. The world had stopped listening to the details.</p><p data-i="25">And the man she loved was there, part of it. She opposed this war with all her heart, and she loved him with all her heart, and the two things didn't fit together in one body.</p><p data-i="26">When the first wave of worry had passed, the call cooled. Short sentences. Silences. &quot;I have to go,&quot; he said in the end. &quot;Me too,&quot; she answered. And neither of them went anywhere.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="27">1 April 2024 · Gaza City</h2><p data-i="28">At the end of March the IDF went into al-Shifa again. Israel said Hamas operatives had regrouped inside the hospital, and hundreds were detained. Two weeks later, when the forces pulled out, the World Health Organization described the place as &quot;an empty shell&quot;.</p><p data-i="29">Fatma stood in front of the gate and didn't go in. Twenty years she had worked there.</p><p data-i="30">&quot;From now on I work wherever there are people,&quot; she said when she got home, and put a first-aid bag on the table. &quot;A school, a tent, a kitchen. It doesn't matter.&quot;</p><p data-i="31">Mehran looked at her, and for the first time he saw that she was old.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="32">13–14 April 2024 · The night of Iran</h2><p data-i="33">April arrived like a wounded animal.</p><p data-i="34">On Saturday night, 13 April, Iran launched its first direct attack on Israel from its own soil: more than three hundred aircraft – explosive drones, cruise missiles and ballistic missiles – in revenge for the killing of senior Revolutionary Guards commanders in Damascus. The drones flew for hours, and the world watched them approach on live maps.</p><p data-i="35">Alex was already deep in the base's concrete shelter when the sirens began. His eyes were fixed on the ceiling, which trembled like a racing pulse.</p><p data-i="36">&quot;This isn't real,&quot; he whispered into the thick air. &quot;This can't be real.&quot;</p><p data-i="37">Around him sat soldiers packed close, smelling of sweat and concrete. Someone read from a phone: Arrow, David's Sling, Iron Dome, American, British, French, Jordanian jets. No one knew how it would end.</p><p data-i="38">It ended faster than they thought. Most of the missiles and drones were shot down before they arrived. In the morning there was talk of ninety-nine per cent interception, of light damage at the Nevatim airbase, and of a seven-year-old Bedouin girl badly hurt by shrapnel. History wrote itself in lines of fire across the sky, and then turned off the light and went to sleep.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="39">That night · Tel Aviv</h2><p data-i="40">Natalie was on her way back from her psychologist when her phone started to beep. The session was supposed to give her a little calm. The sky above her broke.</p><p data-i="41">When she reached the public shelter her heart almost stopped. That sound, the long shriek of the siren, threw her back to the war room, to the smoke. She curled up in a corner like a little girl, hands pressed over her ears. It didn't help. The sound went through her bones. The trauma lived in her body like an animal, and it didn't need ears to hear.</p><p data-i="42">It had happened once before, a week earlier. They were all walking back together from a hostage families' rally at Kaplan, the air full of tyre smoke, and between them, oddly, there was lightness, almost release. Near a little kiosk a boy of about fifteen laughed, too loudly, some stupid remark about somebody's clothes.</p><p data-i="43">And Natalie was already in front of him. Eyes blazing, hands shaking, screaming as if someone had crossed a line there was no coming back from. The boy didn't understand what had hit him. Only when her friends grabbed her from behind and dragged her away did she calm down. And even then her eyes kept searching for a threat that wasn't there.</p><p data-i="44">They walked on, and the silence between them was thick. This was no longer the &quot;tough Natalie&quot; they knew. It was a wild force that burst out of her without warning.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="45">That night · Gaza City</h2><p data-i="46">Mehran sat with the other families in the crowded room, in front of a small television running on a buzzing generator. On Al Jazeera there were streaks of fire in the sky over Jerusalem, interceptions above the Dome of the Rock.</p><p data-i="47">&quot;Now it really starts,&quot; said the mother of one of the children. &quot;Now it's a world war.&quot;</p><p data-i="48">On that same screen he had once watched Hapoel Tel Aviv, in the days when the world was still whole. Now, instead of the green of the grass and the red of the stands, there was only fire and steel. No goals. Only the dead.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="49">Before dawn · Three places</h2><p data-i="50">As night came down on the broken world, all three of them were awake. Alex at the base, between walls that dried out his breath. Natalie in her small flat in Tel Aviv, where memories hung on the walls like faded pictures. Mehran in the crowded house in Gaza, where present and future had melted into one nightmare.</p><p data-i="51">Alex picked up the phone and called Emily. What could he tell her? How do you explain that the sky has fallen? He heard her voice, distant and warm, and the words stuck in his throat. His silence said more than a thousand words.</p><p data-i="52">And Emily, who knew him so well, felt for the first time that she was losing him. Not to death. To something worse: to a trauma eating him from the inside.</p><p data-i="53">She understood, in that moment, that it was over between them. Not because she didn't love him. Because sometimes love isn't enough when the world is falling apart. She chose to swallow it and say nothing. Not to add more weight to shoulders that were already broken.</p><p data-i="54">And Natalie opened, for the first time, the little bag of medical cannabis she had been prescribed. She had resisted it for months. She didn't want to be dependent, didn't want to admit the pain was bigger than her. Now she had no choice.</p><p data-i="55">She lit it. The whitish smoke curled through the air, entered her lungs warm and sweetish, and the world slowly grew softer. The edges less sharp. The pain receding to a murmur.</p><p data-i="56">For a moment, just a moment, she didn't see them.
