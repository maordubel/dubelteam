The sun refused to warm the bones, and the rain didn't just fall, it raged, beating the streets like fists on soft skin.</p><p data-i="2">Every morning Alex woke into a city that had turned strange to him overnight. The streets of Exarchia, which had once felt like home, were full of signs now, shouting at him from every wall: &quot;Free Palestine&quot;, &quot;Occupation = Crime&quot;, &quot;Boycott Israel&quot;. Every sign was a slap.</p><p data-i="3">And he was torn. On one side, the pain of the seventh of October hadn't passed: friends murdered, a sister who had spent long hours inside an outpost that fell, the feeling that terror had won. On the other, he couldn't look at the pictures from Gaza. He had been there. He knew what it was. And still he found it hard to believe his own people were capable of it. Every glance from a passer-by was a question: how do you live with yourself?</p><p data-i="4">At the little café on Stournari Street, where he had sat almost every morning since he came back, the waitress, Anna, no longer smiled at him. Her green eyes, once full of Greek warmth, were as cold as the sea in January. No &quot;kaliméra&quot;, no &quot;ti kaneis&quot;. As if he personally were carrying a gun through the streets of Gaza right now, and not sitting in front of her with a folded newspaper.</p><p data-i="5">&quot;Freddo espresso, métrio,&quot; he ordered, and his voice sounded foreign even to him. Anna took the coins from the table without touching his hand.</p><p data-i="6">In the cold, bitter glass he tasted loneliness. He watched the people passing in the street and felt like a ghost. They were alive. He existed. It wasn't the same thing.</p><p data-i="7">At night the phone rang in the small flat on Alexandras Avenue, a cheap room he had taken when he came back in November, a few streets from Exarchia, which had been his after-work haunt since 2022. His mother.</p><p data-i="8">&quot;Alex? How are you over there?&quot;</p><p data-i="9">How do you tell your mother you feel like a traitor? Worrying her was the last thing he wanted.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="12">January 2025 · Gaza City</h2><p data-i="13">Ahmed was killed at the start of January, ten days before the ceasefire.</p><p data-i="14">A shell, or a missile, nobody could say. He went out to look for flour and didn't come back. By the time Mehran got there, people were already digging with their hands.</p><p data-i="15">The house became a museum of what had been. His father's room still held the smell of tobacco and of the old newspapers he kept in piles by the bed, as if someone would still read them. Mehran didn't dare clear it. It would be like killing him a second time.</p><p data-i="16">Fatma hardly spoke. She went on working at the medical tent, leaving in the morning and coming back in the dark, her hands smelling of disinfectant. Jana cooked over a small fire in the yard. Reem, thirteen now, slept in their father's room, on the floor, among the newspapers.</p><p data-i="17">On the morning of 19 January the ceasefire came into force. After four hundred and seventy-one days. In Gaza, people came out into the streets, some in joy, some only to see what was left.</p><p data-i="18">Mehran didn't go out. But the nights were long, and pride doesn't hear the screaming inside.</p><p data-i="28">In the afternoon she watched three young women climb out of a Red Cross vehicle on television. Romi, Emily, Doron. Four hundred and seventy-one days.</p><p data-i="29">Emily. For a second the name took her to Alex's Emily, the Hungarian with the old cameras, who had packed up her film and left in November, and she wondered whether he was watching too, in Athens, hearing the name.
