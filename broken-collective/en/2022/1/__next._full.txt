The Omicron wave was still sweeping through Israel in January, but for most people it passed faster and lighter than they had feared, and at the beginning of February the Bennett–Lapid government scrapped the Green Pass, which had been the key to all cultural life since February 2021. The masks, the tests, the restrictions – wiped away like footprints on the shore after the tide. Country after country, at the end of winter and into spring, lifted the last of its rules.</p><p data-i="2">Online, mostly on Twitter and Telegram, people began to talk – first in whispers, then out loud – about &quot;the bluff&quot;. Studies showed that the vaccines protected less well against infection with the new variants, and in circles like Alex's that was enough to feel cheated. Others pointed out that the vaccines still prevented serious illness and death. The argument didn't end; it only moved somewhere else. In Israel the cafés filled with laughter again, and with the spring the beaches too, and life slowly came back – with hope, and with heavy questions.</p><h2 class="scene" data-i="3">Athens</h2><p data-i="4">The little bar in Psiri didn't survive the Omicron winter. From the end of December music was banned in Greek nightspots, everything closed at midnight, and nobody was allowed to stand at the bar. &quot;A bar you can't stand at,&quot; said the owner, &quot;is just a room with bottles in it.&quot; At the end of January he pulled down the shutter, and Alex was left with a week's tips and a lot of free time.</p><p data-i="5">In February he found a new job as a barman at Apollon, a bar in Plaka, in the heart of the tourist quarter, below the Acropolis. His hands slid between bottles of ouzo and tsipouro, and every night his smile won over a different woman – a blonde tourist from Sweden on Monday, a dark-haired Greek on Wednesday. Each one a new story of temporary freedom.</p><p data-i="6">Lena was gone by then. In January she had moved to Thessaloniki, to a new band and a room above a rehearsal studio. They parted without drama, with a long hug on the platform at Larissa Station. &quot;Stop searching, Alex,&quot; she told him from the train window. He didn't yet know how that was done.</p><p data-i="7">Anyone in Greece who worked without being vaccinated had to show a negative test twice a week. So twice a week he queued at the pharmacy, paid out of his own pocket, cursed the &quot;health dictatorship&quot; every time the swab went up his nose – and paid again. That was the price of trusting no one.</p><p data-i="8">&quot;This is my life now,&quot; he told himself in the mirror behind the bar. His dark curly hair fell over his grey-green eyes. He was a very good-looking young man, and the alcohol kept a smile on his face at work. But in his heart the cracks remained: the patrols with Golani, the dust and the glass in Rina's flat in Holon in May 2021, his love for Neta, which had dissolved like smoke, and a great many philosophical thoughts about what we are doing in this world as human beings.</p><p data-i="9">That winter he stopped with the pills too. In December a student who had bought Xanax from him mixed it with tsipouro and was taken away by ambulance from Monastiraki Square. The boy survived. Alex went home, tipped everything he had left down the toilet and flushed twice. The next thing, the one that had been waiting for him around the corner, stayed there. He had no wish to find out what a Greek prison looked like from the inside, or what another ambulance looked like either.</p><p data-i="10">His finances were nothing to write home about. Without the money from the pills he got by on a little help from his parents: a bank transfer at the start of every month, and a red heart from Irina in a message. On 12 February he watched the derby that way, on the phone propped against the till: Kobi Refua's Hapoel – Refua had replaced Nir Klinger in December – lost 3–2 to Maccabi at Bloomfield.
