Season 2022 · Chapter 1

The Snake Sheds Its Skin

9 min read

February 2022

The year opened like a long exhalation of relief, as if the world had let out the breath it had been holding for two years. At the beginning of February the Bennett–Lapid government scrapped the Green Pass, which had been the key to all cultural life since 2021. The masks, the tests, the restrictions – wiped away like footprints on the shore after the tide. Country after country, at the end of winter and into spring, lifted the last of its rules.

Online, mostly on Twitter and Telegram, people began to talk – first in whispers, then out loud – about "the bluff". Studies showed that the vaccines protected less well against infection with the new variants, and in circles like Alex's that was enough to feel cheated. Others pointed out that the vaccines still prevented serious illness and death. The argument didn't end; it only moved somewhere else. In Israel the cafés filled with laughter again, the beaches took on the colours of an early summer, and life slowly came back – with hope, and with heavy questions.

Athens

In Athens, Alex found a new job as a barman at Apollon, a bar in Plaka, in the heart of the tourist quarter. His hands slid between bottles of ouzo and tsipouro, and every night his smile won over a different woman – a blonde tourist from Sweden on Monday, a dark-haired Greek on Wednesday. Each one a new story of temporary freedom.

"This is my life now," he told himself in the mirror behind the bar. His dark curly hair fell over his grey-green eyes. He was a very good-looking young man, and the alcohol kept a smile on his face at work. But in his heart the cracks remained: the patrols with Golani, the dust and the glass in Holon in 2021, his love for Neta, which had dissolved like smoke, and a great many philosophical thoughts about what we are doing in this world as human beings.

His finances were nothing to write home about. He got by on a little help from his parents. He watched Hapoel's games on pirate apps – just like Mehran, had he only known. The red shirt with the small crest, Hapoel's hammer and sickle, hung on the wall at home beside the scarf and the basketball vest. It reminded him of home. It reminded him of values.

But Athens was release. Streets lit up in colour, insane street art, Greek music mixing with the laughter of strangers. A normal life, which he tried to drown himself in. Life in Tel Aviv is closed like a ring, he thought – Israel surrounded by enemies, and you can't just get in a car and drive to another country. Here, the idea that you could simply take a car and drive from Greece to Italy enchanted him. His dream was to buy a big van, do it up and travel all over Europe. But city life always won, and the need to earn a living and get ahead prevailed over the dreams he had left in the drawers of his heart.

Tel Aviv

Natalie advanced through the army like an arrow. She had specialised in social media analysis, hours in front of screens, and her commanders had marked her out as a candidate for the officers' course opening in July. Her body had been hardened by training, her mind sharpened.

"I'm rebuilding myself," she told her friend Lihi as they sat over coffee at Dizengoff Center, surrounded by the laughter of girls who had come back to life. There were parties on the beach again, dancing till morning. But beneath the surface she felt something heavy. She wanted to be part of defending Israel, of the future she had dreamed of – even if a deep fear hovered in her heart.

Gaza

In Shati camp, Mehran was building himself a world. An old keyboard stood before him like a digital weapon. The Math Gaza channel, which he had opened in the summer after the war, kept growing: video lessons on equations, short clips about life under the blockade, children playing football in dirt streets.

"The world's going back to normal," he told his mother. Her eyes were tired, but full of pride. "Maybe we'll find a way too."

He wrote to people all over the world and dreamed of freedom beyond the concrete walls. But the hunger and the power cuts reminded him that reality still bit.

24 February 2022

And then everything exploded. Before dawn Vladimir Putin announced on television a "special military operation", claiming he was protecting the Russians of the Donbas. Missiles fell on cities across Ukraine, and armoured columns crossed the border from the north, the east and the south. The world saw an invasion. Within two weeks more than two million people had fled Ukraine – the greatest river of refugees in Europe since the Second World War. Mariupol was being erased under siege, and energy prices in Europe soared.

All around, the West closed in on Russia. Russian banks cut off from SWIFT, oligarchs' assets frozen, one package of sanctions after another. And off to one side, China. Three weeks before the invasion Xi and Putin had declared a partnership with "no limits", and now Beijing refused to condemn. The world split: a West burning with rage, an East growing stronger in the shadows.

The Ben-Gurion home

In the Ben-Gurion family's flat, the tension went off like a bomb from within.

Vitaly, born in St Petersburg, sat in front of the television, his eyes red. He was watching the Russian broadcasts about the "liberation of the Donbas", and the memories came back to him: the cold, the songs of the revolution, the days when the Russians were a power.

"Putin is protecting our people!" he roared. He banged on the table, and a glass of vodka shattered on the floor. "This is a war for honour, not a crime!"

Irina, born in Odesa, rose like a storm. Her face was pale, her tears running like rain. Pictures raced through her head: her grandmother in Kyiv, hiding in a cellar; her uncle, at the front.

"Honour? You call this honour?" she screamed. "They're destroying my home! Ukraine is me, Vitaly! How can you support this?"

She hurled an old plate at the wall, and the china smashed into shards, like memories.

Vitaly tried to reach out a trembling hand. "Irina, I can see your pain…" But his patriotism won. "We have to defend our culture!"

"Please," she begged, "look at the children who are dying." Then she turned, her back to him, and wept.

Natalie stood between them. The green uniform suddenly looked on her like broken armour. "Dad, Mum, stop it!" she shouted. "You're tearing us apart!"

Vitaly turned a furious look on her. "This is none of your business, Natalie. It's a man's war!"

"No!" Irina burst out. "It's the business of every broken family!"

Two people who had lived together for twenty-five years were torn apart in the middle of the living room. Vitaly defending his Russian heritage, Irina weeping for the wounded land of Ukraine. The room filled with shouting, with tears, with memories of a life that had been.

Natalie didn't know which way to turn. "I can't choose!" she wailed, leaning against the wall until she slid down to the floor. Her tears pooled on the cold tiles. She thought about the officers' course, about her dream of defending Israel, and now it all looked like ash. She thought too about what she had seen on monitoring – refugees, crying children – and felt the crack in the family gape open.

"Who am I?" she whispered.

That night · Athens

In Athens, the clink of glasses in the bar fell silent for a moment as convoys of refugees and tanks on snowbound roads appeared on the television. Alex's heart was pounding. The memories of the army came back: the smell of dust, friends shouting. The fear was far away, and yet it was real.

"What's going on there? Are Mum and Dad all right?" he wrote to Natalie.

The answer came later. "Mum and Dad are fighting. I'm falling apart."

He froze, his hand on the vodka bottle. "I'm so far away," he muttered, and wiped away a tear no one saw. He tried to call. The line wouldn't connect. He was left with an empty glass, wondering whether his freedom in Athens was anything more than an illusion.

In Gaza, Mehran saw the same pictures on his phone. The Ukrainian refugees reminded him of his own life. He wrote a post: "Wars break us, but football connects," and added a video of children kicking a ball in a dirt street. A desperate attempt to hold on to hope.

March 2022

The days dragged on, and the cracks in the Ben-Gurion home widened until they could no longer be mended. In the middle of March, after a month of verbal battles, Vitaly and Irina reached the decision that had always been inevitable.

Vitaly, red-eyed and broken-voiced, gathered his things – old photographs, a half-empty bottle of vodka, books of Russian poetry – into an old, torn bag. "I can't do this any more," he muttered, and moved into a small flat in Florentin. There he shut himself away, isolated, like Russia in the Western world.

Irina stayed with Natalie. She sat on the sofa and stared at a family photograph. "I've lost everything," she whispered. The hug she gave Natalie was warm, but without hope.

Natalie sat on the floor by the window. "Mum, how do you carry on after this?"

Irina held her, and cried too. "I've lost my home, your father, and my faith."

That night, in her room, Natalie wrote to Alex: "We're finished." And didn't send it. She was afraid he would break too.

A few days later, she wrote only: "Mum and Dad have split up. I don't know how to go on."

At Apollon, Alex read the message behind the bar. "I have to go back," he whispered. But the fear – of the family, of home, of what would be waiting for him there – kept him tied to the spot.
