## June 2022

The days dragged on, and the family rift was mirrored in a drama larger than itself. In Israel, the government of change – which had been a symbol of hope for Natalie and Alex – lost its majority, one Knesset member after another. On 20 June Bennett and Lapid announced that they were dissolving the Knesset. On 30 June the dissolution passed, and the next day Yair Lapid moved into the prime minister's office, until the election – the fifth in three and a half years – set for 1 November.

In the flat, Natalie heard the news on the television. "Everything's falling apart again," she told Irina, and Irina held her tight. They both cried – for the family, and for the country.

"The government's fallen," she wrote to Alex. "And I don't know if we'll survive this."

"I'm coming back, Sis," he replied.

But he didn't come back. He stood facing the dark sea in Plaka, and felt the world collapsing around him.

## 5–7 August 2022 · Shati camp

In August, the tension in Gaza flared up again. On 5 August Israel launched Operation "Breaking Dawn" against Islamic Jihad, after threats against communities and soldiers along the border. Three days. Some 1,100 rockets were fired from Gaza towards Israel. According to UN figures, 49 Palestinians were killed in Gaza, 17 of them children. Israel claimed that some of them had been killed by Islamic Jihad rockets that fell inside the Strip.

In Shati camp, Mehran sat with the family in the inner room, the ground shaking beneath the explosions. He held his phone and scrolled through pictures of bombed-out houses and children crying in the smoke, and thought of Uncle Yusuf.

"They're breaking us again," he whispered.

On 6 August a missile hit the neighbours' house, and the noise made him scream: "Enough! Enough already!" He ran outside. Clouds of smoke rose over the ruins, small children were crying on the concrete, and bitterness filled his heart. The rift inside him deepened: the wish to defend his place, against the fear of a war that was tearing his soul apart.

That night he wrote an anonymous post: "The world is silent, and the blood keeps flowing."

## September 2022

The darkness that came down on the broken flat in Tel Aviv in September was as heavy as a storm cloud. Silent rooms, the smell of tears and broken memories. Outside the window, the world went on shaking. The Ukrainians broke through around Kharkiv and took back great stretches of land, and more than seven million refugees from Ukraine had already been registered across Europe. Irina watched every report, every map, as if, by looking hard enough, she could make something change.

## October 2022

In October the world began to come apart like a jigsaw no one remembered how to put together.

On 1 October, after hard fighting, Ukrainian forces entered Lyman – a small town nobody had heard of before the war, and a crossroads through which troops and equipment flowed east. The Russians had built defensive lines there that looked impregnable, trenches, and mines sown in the earth like seeds of death. But the Ukrainians, having learned from their victory at Kharkiv, knew that every fortress falls if you press in the right place.

That same night, in Indonesia, mothers waited for children who had gone out to watch a simple football match between Arema and Persebaya, at the Kanjuruhan Stadium in Malang – and never came home. After the game the police fired tear gas into the packed stands, the crowd panicked and ran for the exits, and more than a hundred and thirty people were crushed and suffocated to death. Among them were dozens of children. They knew nothing of the war in Ukraine, nothing of the tension between China and the United States. All they wanted was to see their team win.

On 8 October, in the early hours of the morning, the Crimean Bridge shook under a violent explosion. It was no ordinary bridge: it was Putin's lifeline to the annexed peninsula, a symbol of imperial pride that had cost billions of dollars. Flames rose from the structure, and black smoke stained the sky.

And the tension between Washington and Beijing kept coming to the boil, like a kettle someone had left on the hob. Since August, after Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan, the Chinese navy had been holding exercises around the island that looked more like rehearsals for war than a show of strength. The diplomats talked about "managing differences", and the armies prepared for the moment when words would stop working. Two economies woven into each other – China making everything, America buying everything – threatened to tear apart with one reckless step.

And then came Elon Musk.

On 26 October he walked into Twitter's headquarters in San Francisco carrying a white ceramic sink. "Let that sink in," he wrote. Within minutes the picture had gone viral. The next day the deal closed: $44 billion. He hadn't bought just a platform; he had bought the way humanity talks to itself. The employees looked at the South African billionaire and wondered what would become of them. Some were already getting their CVs ready. He came with plans for mass lay-offs, for changing the algorithms, for lifting restrictions in the name of "free speech" – to reshape the flow of information in a connected world.

The world at the end of October 2022 was not the world that had opened the month. Bridges broken, cities liberated, people dead for the sake of football, superpowers bracing for war, and one man who had bought the square where all of us talk. The broken collective, which had begun to crack in 2020, went on splintering. And now it was no longer clear who was pulling the strings.

## Before the election

In the flat, Natalie sat on the living-room floor wrapped in a blanket, her eyes red. "Mum, what if everything goes back to how it was?"

Irina, her face as pale as snow, squeezed her hand. "I'm frightened, Natalie. Russia is breaking Ukraine, and Israel could fall back into his hands."

She meant Netanyahu. The name still echoed through the house like an open wound. Natalie remembered the cold nights at the protests, and her throat closed.

At Apollon, Alex got a message from her: "The tension here is insane. I don't know what's going to happen." He had heard about the polls, and about his father, who still quietly supported Russia. "If he comes back," he wrote to her, "everything we fought for is gone."

## 1 November 2022

The moment came. The results of the election to the twenty-fifth Knesset struck like lightning: Netanyahu's bloc – 64 seats.

Natalie sat in front of the television, her eyes wide. "No! It can't be!" she shouted, and threw the glass in her hand at the wall. Shards scattered across the floor.

"The change is dead, Natalie," Irina wept beside her. "Everything's going back into the night."

Natalie remembered the protests – the cold, the singing, the flags – and felt a deep betrayal, as if her whole struggle had been trampled underfoot. She collapsed on the floor, sobbing uncontrollably.

In Athens, Alex heard the news on his phone. "Bibi's won," he muttered, and hurled a bottle at the floor behind the bar. He remembered the protests with Natalie, the laughter under the flags, and suddenly it was all far away and lost.

In fury and despair he opened Instagram and posted a story: a red Hapoel flag flying in front of the Acropolis, and across it in huge letters: "FUCK BIBI ONLY HAPOEL".

## That night · Shati camp

In Gaza, Mehran sat in his room by candlelight, after yet another power cut, scrolling through Instagram. Suddenly Alex's story came up on the screen. The words struck him like an echo of his own pain, and he smiled for the first time in days.

Without a second thought, he replied: "FUCK HAMAS ONLY HAPOEL – from a red fan in Gaza!" and added a red heart.

He leaned back. A tear ran down his cheek. Football, he thought. The only shelter in the middle of the chaos.

## December 2022

Alex saw the reply the next morning, and read it three times. A Hapoel fan. From Gaza. He answered in English, something short and full of feeling, and Mehran sent him back a voice message:

"Ahlan, red brother. I'm Mehran. I understand Hebrew and I speak OK, but writing is hard for me, so I'm recording, sorry. I love Hapoel Tel Aviv like you, and I hate my government like you. We were born to suffer, my brother. We are Hapoel."

A shiver of hope ran through Alex's body. Such a small gesture, from someone everyone around him defined as the enemy. But the shared love of the red outweighed everything.

"Ya habibi," he recorded back, "Hapoel Tel Aviv are bigger than Israel and Palestine. We're family. We are Hapoel. You're my brother, because you're Hapoel."

For Mehran, that message felt like a winner in the ninety-third minute. For the first time in his life, for a moment, he had an Israeli friend.

On 10 December Alex wrote to Natalie: "I got a reply from someone in Gaza. A Hapoel fan, his name's Mehran. It's insane. I'm trying to come back, but I'm scared."

"Come," she answered, in tears. "We need you. And this story gives me hope."

She didn't tell him that she already knew the name. That she had been watching him for more than a year, through a different screen.

Irina sat alone in the living room, gazing at a family photograph, feeling the rift with Vitaly and the pain of Ukraine. And Alex, for all his promises, didn't book a ticket. He felt he had finished with Israel the day Bibi came back. He was torn inside, his family was torn apart, and his whole identity was in upheaval.

The world had split. Russia and Ukraine sank into blood, Israel went back into the shadows, and Natalie and Alex's faith in a better future faded away. And yet, between Athens and Shati camp, a single red thread had suddenly been drawn tight.
